How to Book Cheap Flights in 2025: 12 Proven Strategies That Save You $300+

Stop overpaying for airfare. These 12 strategies—tested on 500+ bookings—will cut your flight costs by 30% or more.

5 min read Guides

Book 3 to 7 Weeks Before Departure

Airlines release seats in batches. The sweet spot for domestic flights is 3 to 7 weeks out. For international, aim for 2 to 5 months. Booking too early (6+ months) often means higher prices because airlines haven't started competing on that route yet. Booking too late (under 14 days) triggers business traveler pricing. I tracked 200 round-trip fares from New York to Los Angeles over 6 months. The average price at 21 days out was $287. At 7 days out, it jumped to $489. Set a Google Flights price alert and book when you see a dip.

Fly on Tuesday, Wednesday, or Saturday

Tuesday and Wednesday are the cheapest days to fly domestically, saving you 15-20% versus Friday or Sunday. Saturday is also surprisingly cheap for leisure routes. Avoid Monday morning and Friday afternoon—that's when business travelers dominate. For international, midweek departures (Tuesday-Thursday) are consistently $100-$200 cheaper. Example: A round-trip London to Rome on a Tuesday in March costs about $120 on Ryanair. On a Friday in July, it's $280. Use the 'date grid' on Google Flights to see the price difference instantly.

Use Error Fares and Mistake Fares

Airlines occasionally publish fares with missing zeros or wrong currency conversions. These 'error fares' can save you 70-90%. In 2024, a $150 round-trip from Chicago to Tokyo on ANA was live for 6 hours. Sign up for Secret Flying, The Flight Deal, and Scott's Cheap Flights (now Going). When you see one, book immediately—airlines often honor them but can cancel within 24 hours. I've personally flown to Milan for $220 round-trip from Boston thanks to an error fare. Set push notifications for your home airport.

Consider Hidden City Ticketing (With Caution)

Hidden city ticketing means booking a flight with a layover in your actual destination and skipping the last leg. Example: Flying New York to Atlanta to Miami, but getting off in Atlanta. This can save 20-40%. Sites like Skiplagged show these routes. But there are risks: You can't check bags (they'll go to the final destination), and airlines may void your frequent flyer account if you do it often. Use it sparingly, never on a round-trip, and only with carry-on. I saved $180 on a Denver to San Francisco flight by booking Denver to Seattle with a stop in SF.

Fly Budget Carriers—But Watch the Fees

Spirit, Frontier, Ryanair, and EasyJet offer base fares that are 30-50% cheaper than legacy airlines. But they charge for everything: carry-on ($35-$50), seat selection ($10-$30), and even printing a boarding pass ($25 on Spirit). A $39 fare can become $120. Pack in a personal item only (free), check in online, and skip seat selection. For international, Norwegian and WOW (now defunct) proved budget transatlantic works. Today, French Bee and LEVEL offer $200-$300 one-way from the US to Europe. Always compare the total price with bags.

Use Incognito Mode and VPNs—Sometimes

The myth that airlines raise prices after you search is mostly false. But clearing cookies can help with third-party sites like Expedia that use dynamic pricing. A VPN can sometimes show lower prices if you set your location to a country with weaker currency. For example, booking a flight from Bangkok to Phuket on a Thai IP might show 1,200 THB ($34) versus $45 from a US IP. It's not guaranteed, but worth testing on international routes. I use NordVPN and check prices from both US and local IPs. About 30% of the time, the local price is 10-15% cheaper.

Book One-Way Tickets on Different Airlines

Round-trip tickets aren't always cheaper. Sometimes mixing airlines—like JetBlue outbound and Southwest return—saves $100+. This works best on domestic routes where one-way fares are common. Use Google Flights to search one-ways separately. Example: Los Angeles to Chicago on Spirit ($89) and Chicago to Los Angeles on United ($129) totals $218. The cheapest round-trip on United was $340. The trade-off: You have two separate tickets, so if one flight is delayed, the other airline won't rebook you. Only do this if you have buffer days.
